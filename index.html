Absorption coefficient of lead for gamma radiation

Aim

To determine the absorption coefficient in lead of gamma radiation from the given source.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this session, students should be able to:

  • Know the interaction of radiation with matter.
  • Understand how charged particles interact with matter.
  • Learn how materials are effective in shielding radiation.

Medical perspectives

X-ray imaging and CT

An X-ray image is a map of how much radiation each path through the body absorbs. CT measures this attenuation from many angles and rebuilds it slice by slice.

Tissues absorb differently

Bone, soft tissue, fat and air-filled lung have different attenuation coefficients, which is why they show up as different shades.

Radiation therapy planning

Planning a treatment means predicting how a beam weakens as it passes through tissue, and how much lead or other shielding protects staff and healthy organs.

Theory

Gamma rays are high-energy photons emitted by unstable nuclei. They carry no charge, so they do not slow down gradually like a charged particle. Each photon travels until it has one interaction that removes it from the beam, or it passes straight through.

How gamma photons interact with matter

Charged particles

Alpha and beta particles are charged, so they interact all the time, pulling on electrons along their path and ionising atoms as they go. They lose energy steadily and stop within a definite range: alphas in a few centimetres of air, betas in a few millimetres of aluminium. Gamma photons are indirectly ionising: each interaction sets a fast electron free, and that electron does the ionising. This is also how a Geiger–Müller tube detects gamma rays.

Exponential attenuation

Every thin layer of lead removes the same fraction of the photons that reach it. Equal steps of thickness therefore divide the intensity by equal factors, which gives an exponential fall.

I = I0 e−μx
I0

count rate with no absorber, after background is removed (counts/min)

I

count rate through a thickness x of lead, after background is removed (counts/min)

μ

linear absorption (attenuation) coefficient of the absorber, in cm−1

Example value: μ = 1.20 cm−1 for 662 keV photons in lead.

A straight-line graph

Taking natural logarithms turns the curve into a straight line through the origin:

I0 / I = eμx→ln(I0 / I) = μx

So ln(I0/I) is plotted on the y-axis against thickness x on the x-axis. The slope of the line is μ, in cm−1.

Half-value layer

The thickness that halves the intensity. Setting I = I0/2 gives HVL = ln 2 / μ = 0.693 / μ. With μ = 1.20 cm−1, HVL ≈ 0.58 cm of lead.

Why lead shields well

Lead is dense (11.3 g/cm³) and has a high atomic number (Z = 82), so it has many electrons per centimetre and a strong photoelectric effect. Dividing μ by density gives the mass attenuation coefficient μ/ρ in cm²/g, which compares materials fairly.

The Geiger–Müller counter

A GM tube is a gas-filled cylinder with a thin wire along its axis held at a high positive voltage. A gamma photon frees an electron in the wall or the gas; the electron ionises the gas, and near the wire the field is strong enough to set off an avalanche along the whole tube. Each avalanche gives one pulse, one count, whatever the energy of the photon.

The scaler counts the pulses for a preset time. After each pulse the tube needs a short dead time (around 100 µs) before it can count again, which matters only at very high rates.

The operating voltage is chosen on the flat plateau, here about 500 V, where a small drift in voltage hardly changes the count rate.

Background and random counts

Background radiation

Even with no source nearby the tube counts cosmic rays and natural radioactivity in rocks, building materials and air, typically a few tens of counts per minute. This background IBG is measured first and subtracted from every reading, so that I0 and I describe the source alone.

Counts are random

Radioactive decay is random, so repeated one-minute counts differ. For a count N the natural spread is about √N (Poisson statistics): about ±52 for 2700 counts. Repeating each count three times and averaging reduces this scatter.

Method

Apparatus

Steps

Safety
  • Time, distance, shielding. Keep the source out of its container for as short a time as possible, stay back from it, and keep lead between the source and people.
  • Handle the source with tongs only, never with fingers, and hold it at arm's length.
  • Never point the source at anyone, including the eyes, and never look straight down the open face of the source.
  • Return the source to its lead container as soon as the counts are done.
  • Lead is toxic. Handle the sheets with gloves or wash hands afterwards; no eating or drinking at the bench.
Precautions for good results
  • Work on the plateau. Raise the voltage slowly and do not go above the plateau: continuous discharge can damage the tube.
  • Keep the geometry fixed. The source-to-tube distance must not change between readings; only the absorber changes.
  • Absorbers midway and square to the beam, so the photons cross exactly the thickness x.
  • Repeat and average. Three one-minute counts for every setting, because single counts scatter by about √N.
  • Background first. Measure it with the source safely in its container, and subtract it from every average.
  • Keep other sources away from the bench so the background stays the same throughout.

Common slips

Background not subtracted

Using 3412 instead of 3388 for I0, and Iav instead of I, slightly bends the line; the error grows as the count rate falls towards the background.

log10 instead of ln

log10(2.050) = 0.312, not 0.718. Every value is 2.303 times too small, and so is μ.

Ratio upside down

ln(I/I0) = −0.718. A negative logarithm means I and I0 were swapped.

Slope upside down

Δx ÷ Δy = 0.80 ÷ 0.96 = 0.83 cm. The slope is rise over run: Δy ÷ Δx.

Axes swapped

Plotting x on the y-axis gives a slope of 1/μ. Keep ln(I0/I) on the y-axis.

HVL upside down

μ ÷ 0.693 = 1.73 has the wrong unit. HVL = 0.693 ÷ μ, in cm.
